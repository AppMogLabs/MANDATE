# Onboarding AI-naive players to write mandates that work

**The core challenge MANDATE faces isn't teaching AI — it's teaching delegation.** Every product that has successfully onboarded users to autonomous AI systems converges on the same insight: users don't need to understand how AI works, they need to understand how to communicate intent clearly and build calibrated trust in an autonomous executor. The most effective onboarding patterns come not from AI products alone but from the intersection of prompt engineering UX, autonomous agent design, and decades of "director games" that already teach players to think in instructions rather than actions. MANDATE's natural-language mandate editor is, in UX terms, a prompt engineering interface wrapped in a strategic delegation game — and both domains offer battle-tested patterns for exactly this problem.

The research below synthesizes findings from consumer AI onboarding (ChatGPT, Claude, Midjourney, Copilot), autonomous agent UX (Devin, Cursor, Replit Agent, Claude Code), programming and delegation games (Screeps, Zachtronics, Gladiabots, Majesty, Football Manager, auto-battlers), and academic research on AI misconceptions and trust calibration.

## Half of your players will struggle to write what they mean

Jakob Nielsen's research identifies the **"Articulation Barrier"** — the gap between what users truly need and what they can express in words. Roughly half of adults in advanced countries are classified as low-literacy users who struggle with complex descriptive prose. This isn't about intelligence; it's about the specific skill of translating strategic intent into precise written instructions. A NN/g usability study found that **46% of first-time AI prompts had "missed opportunities"** — users wrote vague instructions like "fix it" or "do well" without specifying what success looked like.

This maps directly to MANDATE's central design tension. A mandate like "do well" gives the AI agent nothing actionable. A mandate like "prioritize acquiring iron ore, trading surplus wheat at no less than 2:1 ratios, and avoid deals with players who have broken agreements" gives the agent a workable strategy. The gap between these two players will be enormous — and the vague-mandate player won't understand why they're losing.

The products that solve this best don't rely on documentation. They scaffold the writing process through UI. Nielsen identified six prompt augmentation patterns that directly apply to a mandate editor:

**Style galleries** replace blank-page anxiety with browsable options. Midjourney's Explore page lets users see others' prompts alongside results. In MANDATE, a gallery of successful mandates from previous games — anonymized, with outcome scores — would teach by example far more effectively than any tutorial text.

**Prompt rewrite** is the pattern where AI enhances a simple input into a richer one. Leonardo AI's "prompt enhance" takes "A robot looking puzzled" and generates a full paragraph of detail. For MANDATE, a player typing "be aggressive" could see a suggested expansion: "Initiate trades proactively. Accept slightly unfavorable terms to build inventory in the first three rounds. Prioritize military resources over luxury goods. Target weaker players for negotiation." The player edits from there. Research consistently shows it's easier to edit existing text than to create from scratch.

**Prompt builders** use structured form elements — dropdowns, sliders, fill-in-the-blanks — to replace free-text composition. Google Imagen's Prompt Builder offers categorized dropdowns. ChatGPT Canvas provides reading-level sliders. For MANDATE's beginner mode, a structured mandate builder could offer: Priority Resource [dropdown] → Trading Aggressiveness [slider] → Alliance Preference [dropdown] → Hard Constraints [checklist]. This generates a natural-language mandate the player can then customize.

**Parametrization** — hybrid UI combining text with GUI controls — is the pattern Midjourney perfected. Players start with pure text, then discover that appending `--ar 16:9` changes the aspect ratio. In MANDATE, mandate parameters could work similarly: write your strategy in plain text, then toggle advanced modifiers like risk tolerance, time horizon, or conditional triggers through UI controls that append structured clauses to the mandate.

## The setup-execute-analyze loop is your core teaching mechanism

Auto-battlers have already proven that the pattern at MANDATE's heart — write instructions, watch autonomous execution, analyze results, iterate — can be deeply engaging. In Teamfight Tactics, players buy and position champions during a preparation phase, then watch fully autonomous combat. The learning curve from "I want to control my units" to "I need to build the right system" happens naturally through the two-phase structure itself.

The critical insight from Zachtronics games is their **histogram feedback system**. After solving a puzzle, players see aggregate histograms showing how their solution compares to all other players across multiple metrics. This replaced global leaderboards and solved two problems: leaderboards incentivize cheating, and leaderboards tell most players they're bad. Histograms let players "discover that their solution is terrible, but quickly formulate a personal challenge to do better." For MANDATE, showing how a player's mandate performed relative to others — not a single ranking, but a distribution curve across metrics like resource efficiency, diplomatic success, and military outcomes — would drive intrinsic motivation to iterate without creating shame spirals.

Gladiabots offers the most directly applicable feedback pattern. Players construct AI behavior trees for robot squads, then watch autonomous combat. The critical UX element: **selecting a unit during replay shows the state of its AI tree in real-time** — which conditions were evaluated, which actions triggered, and why. Players can see exactly where their logic broke down. MANDATE needs an equivalent: after each round, players should be able to click on any agent action and see which part of their mandate the agent was following, what conditions it evaluated, and what alternative actions it considered.

The Zachtronics design postmortem for SpaceChem contains a warning directly relevant to MANDATE: "We used too much text to explain things. In retrospect, this is a clear sign that we needed to change the game, not explain it more forcefully." If players don't understand how mandates work, the answer isn't more tutorial text — it's better feedback loops that make cause and effect visible.

## Progressive disclosure turns complexity into a learning curve

Every successful AI product and delegation game converges on the same structural approach: start absurdly simple, then reveal complexity at the moment the player needs it.

Midjourney exemplifies this with five natural tiers. Level zero: type any words, get four images — zero parameters needed. Level one: learn aspect ratio (`--ar 16:9`) — one parameter, huge impact. Level two: discover style references. Level three: explore fine-tuning parameters. Level four: multi-prompt weighting with `::` syntax. Level five: image prompting, blending, inpainting. Each tier builds on the last, and players naturally progress when their current tier stops satisfying them.

RimWorld teaches delegation through a parallel progression. The game starts in automatic mode — colonists assign their own work priorities via simple checkboxes. New players eventually discover the system's limitations (the doctor is hauling rocks instead of treating the wounded) and switch to manual priority mode, learning to use a 1-4 priority scale. Advanced players then layer in allowed areas, schedules, and zone restrictions. The community-created "You Do You" mod is particularly illuminating for MANDATE: it removes the work tab entirely and uses rule-based AI to assign priorities based on colonist traits, skills, mood, and needs — colonists "want to do things they are good at" and "pursue passions if in good mood." This is essentially a prototype of what MANDATE's AI interpretation layer could look like.

For MANDATE's mandate editor, progressive disclosure should follow this concrete sequence:

- **Tier 1 (Tutorial rounds)**: Single-sentence mandates with one clear objective. "Focus on gathering wood." The game provides template mandates the player modifies rather than writing from scratch.
- **Tier 2 (Early game)**: Multi-clause mandates with conditions. "If attacked, prioritize defense; otherwise, expand territory." The UI introduces an "Add Condition" button.
- **Tier 3 (Mid game)**: Priority-weighted mandates with resource constraints. "Acquire iron (high priority) and stone (medium priority), maintaining at least 500 gold in reserve." Sliders and constraint tools appear.
- **Tier 4 (Advanced)**: Multi-domain mandates with contingency planning and diplomatic stance. The full mandate editor unlocks, including conditional logic, agent personality tuning, and inter-round persistence controls.

Football Manager's layered instruction system is a direct model: Team Instructions set overall style ("play out of defense," "higher tempo"), Position Instructions define role-specific behavior, and Player Instructions create individual overrides. MANDATE could use an identical hierarchy — global strategy → domain-specific instructions (trade, military, diplomacy) → situational overrides.

## Trust requires transparency, reversibility, and player-initiated control

Research on delegation anxiety reveals a critical psychological finding: **when an AI system proactively offers to take over tasks, users experience "perceived self-threat"** — a sense that their competence is being challenged. This decreases willingness to delegate. But when users initiate delegation themselves, the effect reverses — delegation actually improves human satisfaction and self-efficacy. The implication for MANDATE is foundational: the player must always feel like the strategist who chooses to delegate, never like a passive observer watching an AI play the game.

The autonomous agent products that build trust most effectively share six UX patterns, identified by Smashing Magazine's 2026 framework for agentic AI design:

**Intent preview** is non-negotiable. Before execution, the agent states what it plans to do. Devin, Claude Code, and Replit Agent all implement this as a plan review step. In MANDATE, after submitting a mandate, the agent should present its interpretation: "Based on your mandate, I plan to: (1) Offer wheat to Players 2 and 4 at 2:1 for iron, (2) Reject any trade below 1.5:1, (3) Stockpile surplus if no favorable deals emerge." The player approves, modifies, or rewrites. The Smashing Magazine framework's target: **greater than 85% approval without edits**. If more than 10% of players consistently override the interpretation, the mandate parsing needs improvement.

**The autonomy dial** lets users set their comfort level per domain. Cursor pioneered this with an explicit slider from manual to fully autonomous. For MANDATE, the dial should be per-domain: Advisory mode (agent only suggests), Supervised mode (agent proposes plans for each action), Delegated mode (agent executes within constraints, flags exceptions), and Full Autonomy (agent operates independently, reports after). New players start in Supervised mode; the game progressively encourages moving toward Delegated as trust builds.

**Checkpoint and rollback** is the single most effective mechanism for reducing delegation anxiety. Claude Code automatically saves state before each change; users can instantly rewind by tapping Esc twice. Replit Agent creates checkpoints throughout execution; users can roll back to any prior state. The mere existence of undo — even if rarely used — creates psychological safety. MANDATE should auto-save game state before each major agent decision, letting players rewind to any checkpoint.

**Explainable rationale** prevents valid autonomous actions from being perceived as bugs. Every AI action should be inspectable. The Smashing Magazine framework specifies: justifications should be grounded in the user's own stated preferences. "I traded wheat for iron at 1.8:1 because your mandate specified 'acquire iron as top priority' and no 2:1 offers were available." This ties outcomes directly to mandate quality, reinforcing that the player — not the agent — drives results.

**Confidence signals** prevent both over-trust and under-trust. Display the agent's certainty level: a green indicator for "high confidence: enemy forces are weak on the eastern front" versus a yellow indicator for "low confidence: diplomatic outcome uncertain — recommend player review." Research from a 2025 ArXiv paper identified the **Trust Calibration Paradox**: users simultaneously over-trust AI through automation bias and under-trust it through algorithm aversion. Confidence signals help calibrate by making uncertainty visible.

Devin's UX deserves special attention as a model. Its "Following" mode auto-switches between workspace tabs (shell, browser, editor, planner) as the AI uses them — users literally watch the AI work like watching a colleague's screen share. A time-travel scrubber lets users drag through the agent's activity history. Instead of streaming text character by character, Devin shows "Devin is typing..." then posts complete messages — mimicking human chat behavior. Product analyst Paolo Perazzo calls it "one of the most well-conceived AI products" in terms of trust-building UX. For MANDATE, a "war room" view where players can watch the agent negotiate, trade, and manage resources in real-time — with the ability to scrub backward through the round's history — would build the same trust.

## Five misconceptions that will break MANDATE gameplay if unaddressed

Research across academic studies, UX research, and industry analysis identifies specific misconceptions that would most damage MANDATE's core loop. These should be addressed in priority order.

**Players will expect deterministic execution.** A study running the same LLM prompt 1,000 times at temperature=0 produced **80 unique completions**. Players writing identical mandates in consecutive rounds will be shocked when outcomes differ. The fix: the tutorial should explicitly show the same mandate running three times with slightly different outcomes, framed as "Your agent interprets your strategy freshly each round — like a human advisor would." Show a range of likely outcomes when previewing a mandate, not a single predicted result. Reframe mandates as strategies with variance, not programs with deterministic output.

**Players won't realize prompt quality is the game.** The EPIC study found users approach AI through four pre-existing mental models — search engines, rule-based chatbots, voice assistants, and recommendation systems — none of which prepare them for a system where input quality determines output quality. Harvard and MIT Sloan both emphasize that "the granularity of your input is directly proportional to the utility of the output." The most effective fix is a **Round Zero calibration**: show the agent acting with no mandate (random, chaotic behavior), then with a good mandate (strategic, purposeful behavior). This single demonstration makes the player's role tangible immediately. Follow it with a side-by-side comparison of a vague mandate ("do well") versus a specific one, with outcome scores, so the quality gap is viscerally obvious.

**Players will think constraints limit rather than improve output.** Research shows role-based prompts improved AI output relevance by **34%**, and constraint-based prompting improved adherence to requirements by **25%** in professional tasks. Yet new players will write broad, open-ended mandates thinking they're giving the agent "freedom to be creative." Figma's blog captures the principle: "Specificity doesn't mean rigidity. It's precision paired with adaptability." The game should reward specificity mechanically — perhaps through a visible "Mandate Clarity" score that correlates with outcome quality, or an achievement system that rewards using specific constraint patterns like resource floors, conditional logic, and diplomatic red lines.

**Players won't iterate.** OpenAI's own guidance states "AI engineering is inherently an empirical discipline." Yet most users write one prompt and accept or reject the output. MANDATE's entire learning loop depends on iterative mandate improvement. The fix is structural: after each round, the game should present a post-round revision screen showing what happened, highlighting which mandate clauses drove which outcomes, and prompting "Want to refine your mandate based on what happened?" A mandate history with diff view — showing how mandates evolved and correlating changes with performance — makes iteration feel natural rather than effortful.

**Players will anthropomorphize the agent.** NN/g identified four degrees of anthropomorphism in AI interaction: courtesy (saying "please"), reinforcement (praising the AI), roleplay (assigning personas), and companionship (emotional bonding). Anthropomorphic attributions to AI increased **34% year-over-year** through 2025. Players will write mandates as if talking to a person: "Please be careful and don't let them take advantage of us." This emotional, subjective language is nearly impossible for an AI to operationalize meaningfully. The UI should use mechanical language — "your agent," not "your advisor" — and when emotional mandates are detected, surface a translation prompt: "Translate to: Reject any trade where we give more value than we receive?"

## Majesty's lesson: make delegation feel like power, not limitation

The 2001 game Majesty remains the purest precedent for MANDATE's design philosophy. The player is a ruler who cannot directly control hero units — instead, you place bounties and build infrastructure to incentivize autonomous heroes. Heroes have class-specific AI personalities: Paladins are brave and attack; Rogues prioritize money; Rangers scout. You set bounties in 100 or 500 gold increments, and even then, cooperation isn't guaranteed.

The genius of Majesty's design is its framing. The game's central conceit — you're the quest-assigning ruler, the NPC who gives quests in RPGs, not the hero — makes indirect control feel narratively natural rather than artificially limiting. When Paradox made Majesty 2, they received criticism for making heroes "too controllable," undermining the core appeal. Community analysis concludes: "Paradox mistook Majesty for an RTS when it's really a city-builder. Most of the fun comes from building up an intricate society and watching your heroes go about their own business."

This contains a profound lesson for MANDATE. The game must frame the player as a powerful strategist who delegates because that's what powerful strategists do — not as someone arbitrarily prevented from clicking buttons. Research on delegation psychology confirms this: framing AI delegation around the player's strategic identity ("you are the general; the agent is your staff") rather than around the AI's capabilities reduces delegation anxiety and increases satisfaction. Every AI action should reinforce outcome ownership: "Your strategy succeeded because your mandate anticipated the resource shortage."

Duskers offers a complementary model. Players type CLI commands to control drones exploring derelict spaceships. Developer Tim Keenan explained the design choice: "If Duskers had a polished mouse-driven interface I'd probably try to play it like a twitch RTS... the command line interface signals the correct way for a player to approach playing the game." The deliberate friction of text input created tension that direct control wouldn't have. Duskers also introduced an alias system where players could define command shortcuts — essentially writing reusable instruction macros. MANDATE should let players save, name, and reuse mandate templates, building a personal library of strategies over time.

## A concrete onboarding sequence for MANDATE

Synthesizing all research findings into a specific, implementable onboarding flow:

**Round Zero — "The Agent Without a Mandate."** Before the player writes anything, show the agent acting with no instructions. It makes random trades, accepts bad deals, ignores threats. Then overlay a well-written mandate and replay the same scenario. The agent now trades strategically, stockpiles resources, and rejects unfavorable terms. This single demonstration establishes three critical mental models simultaneously: the player's mandate is what matters, the agent is capable but needs direction, and mandate quality directly determines outcomes. Google's People + AI Guidebook recommends exactly this pattern: "This is your agent, and it'll help you by executing your strategy. Right now, it needs your instructions to act effectively. You can improve its performance by writing clearer, more specific mandates."

**Round One — "Edit, Don't Create."** Provide a pre-written template mandate appropriate to the scenario. The player modifies it rather than writing from scratch, lowering the articulation barrier. Show the agent's interpretation of the mandate before execution (the intent preview pattern). After execution, show a simple outcome report linking mandate clauses to agent actions.

**Round Two — "Your Turn to Write."** The player writes their own mandate from scratch, with a structured builder available as scaffolding (Priority Resource dropdown, Trading Aggressiveness slider, Hard Constraints checklist). Show the outcome alongside the Round One template result for comparison. Introduce the mandate clarity score.

**Round Three — "The Iteration Round."** After Round Two results, prompt the player to refine their mandate. Highlight which clauses worked and which didn't. Show the diff between the original and revised mandate. This establishes the iterate-and-improve habit early.

**Round Four — "Constraints Are Power."** Present a scenario where an unconstrained mandate ("trade for resources") produces mediocre results. Then show how adding three specific constraints ("trade wheat only when reserves exceed 10; reject iron trades below 2:1; prioritize deals with Player 3") dramatically improves performance. This is the moment players learn that specificity beats generality.

**Ongoing — "The War Room."** From Round Five onward, the full mandate editor is available with progressive features unlocking as the player demonstrates competence. The war room dashboard shows a real-time activity feed of agent actions, an agent confidence indicator for each decision, a "why did you do that?" inspection button on every action, a histogram showing mandate performance relative to other players, and mandate history with diff view correlating changes to outcomes.

This sequence addresses every major misconception identified in the research — prompt quality matters (Round Zero), constraints improve output (Round Four), iteration is essential (Round Three), the agent is a tool not a mind-reader (Round One's interpretation preview), and outcomes vary (visible across all rounds). It uses progressive disclosure to avoid overwhelming new players while creating a natural learning curve from template-editing to free-form mandate authorship. And it frames the player as a powerful strategist from the very first moment, ensuring delegation feels like an expression of authority rather than a loss of control.
